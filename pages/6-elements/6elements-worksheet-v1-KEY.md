## 1 — PITCH

**1.1** **b) frequency**

**1.2** Pitch gets **higher**. Wavelength gets **shorter**.
*Because:* accept anything that connects the two — "faster waves are shorter,"
"more waves fit in the same space," "higher frequency means shorter wavelength."

> **Teacher note:** This is the one kids get backwards. Higher pitch = *more* waves
> per second = *less* room for each one. If a kid says "longer," have them draw two
> waves side by side before you correct them.

**1.3** **Stays the same.** Hold position = hold frequency. The page only changes
pitch when the finger *moves*.

**1.4** **CREDIT IF** they name two real sound sources and the high/low assignment is
actually correct. Banned: "a bird," "a truck." Good answers: smoke alarm, tea kettle,
mosquito / thunder, a bass drop, a foghorn, a big dog.

## 2 — VOLUME

**2.1** **b) volume**

**2.2** Circle **"the amplitude got bigger"** and **"the pitch stayed the same."**
Both, not one.

**2.3** Accept: **independent · separate · not the same thing · two different elements ·
you can change one without the other.**

**2.4** **CREDIT IF** they say the jump scare *increases the amplitude suddenly* AND
give a reason it works — surprise, contrast, your ears were relaxed, you turned it up
to hear the quiet part. The contrast idea is the one worth praising out loud.

**3.3**
**Different:** duration — how long it lasts / one is over quick, one is held.
**Same:** the pitch (also the volume and the timbre — accept any of them).

## 4 — SPACE

**4.1** **b) sound combined with the reflections of its environment**

**4.2** PAN = **left and right.** REVERB = **closer and farther.**

**4.3** Looking for: reverb adds **reflections** that arrive *after* and *around* the
direct sound. More reflections compared to direct sound = your brain reads "farther
away" or "bigger room." Full credit for "the reflections make it sound like it's coming
from across a big room" as long as the word *reflections* is in there.

**4.4** **Keep them all on the left.**

> **Teacher note — verified in the code.** The pan control sits *before* the delay in
> the signal path, and the delay is mono. Whatever you pan, the echoes follow. This is
> the good "predict and get it wrong" item — most kids guess the echoes bounce
> side to side, because that's what delay does in movies and video games.
> Being wrong here is the lesson: **signal flow order decides what happens.**
> Point them at the SIGNAL FLOW switch right after.

**4.5** **CREDIT IF** they name a real space and a physical reason. Gym, stairwell,
bathroom, church, parking garage, empty hallway, indoor pool. Reason should be about
hard flat surfaces, high ceilings, or emptiness — anything the sound bounces off.

## 5 — TIMBRE

**5.1** **a) the lowest, loudest frequency in a sound**

**5.2** Fundamental = **far left.** Every other fader = **overtone.**

**5.3**
**Changed:** timbre.
**Did not change:** pitch.
**How they know:** the fundamental (the far-left fader, the lowest frequency) never
moved — and pitch is set by the fundamental. Accept "it's still the same note, it just
sounds like a different instrument."

**5.4** Circle **"all the frequencies moved up together"** and **"the timbre stayed
about the same."**
Do **not** circle "still 100 Hz apart" or "became a different instrument."

> **Teacher note — verified in the code.** Every partial is set to
> *(its number) × 100 Hz × MASTER PITCH.* So turning MASTER PITCH up **multiplies**
> them all — the spacing spreads (100/200/300 becomes 200/400/600 at 2×).
> The *ratios* stay locked, which is exactly why the timbre survives.
> **This is the biggest idea on the page:** timbre lives in the *ratios* between
> frequencies, not in the frequencies themselves. Same reason a trumpet still sounds
> like a trumpet whether it plays high or low.

**5.5**
LOW PASS down → **darker**, removes the **high** frequencies.
HIGH PASS up → **thinner**, removes the **low** frequencies.

> **Teacher note:** the names are backwards from what kids expect. A "low pass" filter
> *passes the lows through* and cuts the highs. Say it as "**low pass = lows may
> pass**" and it sticks.

**5.6** **CREDIT IF** they used two of *fundamental / overtone / frequency / partials*
and none of the banned words. The answer itself can be anything — hollow, nasal, like a
phone speaker, like it's underwater, scooped out, like someone talking through a pipe.
Full credit for "some of the overtones are missing so it sounds hollow."

## 6 — ENVELOPE

**6.1** **b) the change in a value over time**

**6.2**
- **S** — the value while you are still holding the trigger
- **A** — the first increase, right when the trigger turns on
- **R** — the final decrease, after you let go
- **D** — the first decrease, right after the peak

**6.3** Circle **S and R**? **No — circle R only... and S is the trap.**

> **Correction to the item, not the kids.** As written, 6.3 says "two happen while the
> trigger is ON, two happen after it goes OFF." That is **wrong** — **A, D, and S all
> happen while the trigger is ON. Only R happens after you let go.** That's a 3/1
> split, not 2/2.
>
> Fix it before you print. Change 6.3 to:
>
> > **6.3** Three of these happen while the trigger is **ON**. Only one happens after
> > it goes **OFF**. Circle the one that happens after you let go: **A D S R**
>
> Answer: **R.**

**6.4** First one (attack down/fast) = **hit** a drum.
Second one (attack up/slow) = **bowed** a violin.
*(Accept "slid into" for the second — it's describing the same thing.)*

**6.5 — CAR HORN**
Attack: **down** (instant) · Decay: **down or none** · Sustain: **up (full)** ·
Release: **down** (stops immediately)

> Accept any wording that means fast-on, hold-full, fast-off. The Sustain must be **up** —
> that's the whole point of "holds forever."

**6.6 — THUNDER**
Attack: **up** (slow build) · Decay: **middle** · Sustain: **middle** ·
Release: **up** (long roll away)

**Why they changed Release:** because the thunder keeps rolling *after* the strike is
over. Release controls what happens once the trigger is off. Accept "so it fades out
slow instead of stopping."

**6.7** **It still affects the sound, just very slowly.** The LFO never stops — a slow
LFO is still moving, you just have to watch the screens to see it.

**6.8** **CREDIT IF** they pick a side and name a use. Useful answers: a siren, a
wobble bass, a helicopter, making a held note breathe so it isn't boring, a slow
filter sweep in a build-up. "Annoying" is a fine answer if they say *why* — e.g. it
never lets the sound sit still.

## FINAL

**F.1**
A hand clap vs. a hand clap in an empty gym → **SPACE**
A trumpet and a flute, same note, same loudness, same length → **TIMBRE**

> These two are the whole unit in one question. If a kid gets both, they have it.

**F.2** **CREDIT IF** all three elements are named correctly and each one is tied to
something actually in the song. "The volume is loud" = half credit; push for
*"the volume drops out completely before the chorus so the chorus hits harder"* —
that's the answer you want, and it's worth reading out loud.

**F.3** **No wrong answer.** Full credit for any element plus a defense that uses the
vocabulary. No credit for "idk," "all of them," or a pick with no reason.

> **The defenses worth hearing:**
> **Timbre** — it can turn the same note into a completely different instrument.
> **Envelope** — it decides whether something is a drum or a violin.
> **Space** — it moves the sound somewhere else without touching the sound itself.
> **Volume** — turn it to zero and every other element stops mattering.
>
> That last one is a genuinely strong argument. If a kid makes it, say so.
